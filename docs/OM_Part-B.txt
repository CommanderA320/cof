0. ADMINISTRATION and CONTROL  0.1. Introduction  A)   Turkish Airlines hereby certifies that Turkish Airlines   EK.10.73.005 Operations Manual Part-B Airbus A320 has been established in compliance with Turkish DGCA/EASA regulations and with the terms and the conditions of the Turkish Airlines Air Operator Certificate.  B)   This operations manual contains operational instructions that are to be complied with by the relevant personnel.  C)   The Turkish Airlines   EK.10.73.005 Operations Manual Part-B Airbus 320 consists of chapters stated in AMC3 ORO.MLR.100, B Aircraft Operating Matters – Type Related. Applicability and use of   EK.10.73.005 Operations Manual Part-B Airbus 320 as follows:  i)   EK.10.73.005 Operations Manual Part-B Airbus 320 is designed and developed in accordance with the guidelines of safe operating policies, procedures and techniques as published by manufacturers or authorities for all and specific airplane types currently operated by Turkish Airlines.  ii)   EK.10.73.005 Operations Manual Part-B Airbus 320 defines general and specific guidelines, procedures, policies and techniques related to A320 aeroplanes.  iii)   Document hierarchy is defined in   PR.10.18.039 Procedure For Management of Corporate Documents. All flights shall be planned and operated in accordance with: Turkish Airlines Regulations and Directives, and Turkish Airlines   EK.10.73.001 Operations Manual Part-A, Turkish Airlines   EK.10.73.005 Operations Manual Part-B, Turkish Airlines   EK.10.73.002 Operations Manual Part-C, Turkish Airlines   EK.10.72.001 Operations Manual Part-D, and Other Turkish Airlines approved/published/distributed relevant documents and manuals. When the documentation listed in above are contradictory to rules and regulations established and mandated by State Authorities, the more restrictive shall be applicable until revisions can be implemented in the appropriate documentation.  iv)   The following manuals override (more restrictive of all applicable) in the event of a conflict with the   EK.10.73.005 Operations Manual Part-B Airbus 320 : Turkish DGCA/EASA Regulations, ICAO Annexes (if applicable), FAA Regulations and/or Requirements (if applicable), and Airplane Flight Manuals.  v)   Any discrepancy, error or difficulty noted shall be reported to the   Flight Standards Management.  vi)   All operations personnel shall be familiar and comply with the laws, regulations, standards and procedures of the states in which operations are conducted and with the contents of this manual relevant to the performance of their duties. The standards and procedures outlined in the   EK.10.73.005 Operations Manual Part-B Airbus 320 shall be adhered to at all times. Nothing contained in this manual shall preclude personnel from acting in their own best judgement in cases of emergency or during any irregularity for which no provisions may have been made within this manual. All Turkish Airlines flight crew is responsible and it is mandatory to follow memos, bulletins and e-mails distributed by the Flight Operations Directorate and flight crew related documents and applications. An aerodrome on which the aeroplane can be operated, taking account of the applicable performance requirements and runway characteristics.  Aerodrome Operating Minima  “Aerodrome Operating Minima” means the limits of usability of an aerodrome for: Take-off, expressed in terms of runway visual range (RVR) and/or visibility and, if necessary, ceiling; Landing in 2D instrument approach operations, expressed in terms of visibility and/or RVR, minimum descent altitude/height (MDA/H) and, if necessary, ceiling; Landing in 3D instrument approach operations, expressed in terms of visibility and/or RVR and decision altitude/height (DA/H) as appropriate to the type and/or category of the operation;  Aeroplane  A power driven, heavier than air aircraft, deriving its lift in flight chiefly from aerodynamic reactions on surfaces which remain fixed under given conditions of flight.  Aeroplane Flight Manual  A manual associated with the certificate of airworthiness that contains information (limits, procedures, data, etc.) required to operate the aeroplane at the level of safety established by the aeroplane's certification basis.  Air Operator Certificate (AOC)  A certificate authorizing an operator to carry out specified commercial air transport operations.  Aircraft  Any machine that can derive support in the atmosphere from the reactions of the air other than the reactions of the air against the earth's surface, it includes aeroplanes and helicopters.  Aircraft Variant  As used with respect to the licensing and operation of flight crew, means an aircraft of the same basic certificated type which contain modifications not resulting in significant changes of handling and/or flight characteristic, or flight crew complement, but causing significant changes to equipment and/or procedures.  Alternate Aerodrome  An adequate aerodrome to which an aircraft may proceed when it becomes either impossible or inadvisable to proceed to or to land at the aerodrome of intended landing where the necessary services and facilities are available, where aircraft performance requirements can be met and which is operational at the expected time of use. Alternate aerodromes include the following:  Take-off alternate -An alternate aerodrome at which an aircraft can land should it become necessary shortly after take-off and it is not possible to use the aerodrome of departure.  En-route alternate-   An aerodrome at which an aircraft would be able to land after experiencing an abnormal or emergency condition during en-route phase of the flight.  Fuel/Energy Enroute Alternate -(fuel/energy ERA) aerodrome means an ERA Aerodrome that is required at the planning stage for use in the calculation of fuel/energy  Destination alternate -An alternate aerodrome to which an aircraft may proceed should it become impossible or inadvisable to land at the aerodrome of intended landing. Note: Note  Note:  The aerodrome from which a flight departs may also be an en-route or a destination alternate aerodrome for that flight.  Approved by the Authority  Documented by the Authority as suitable for the purpose intended. Augmented Flight Crew  A flight crew which comprises more than the minimum number required to operate the aeroplane, allowing each flight crew member to leave the assigned post, for the purpose of inflight rest, and to be replaced by another appropriately qualified flight crew member.  Authority  The competent body responsible for the safety regulation of civil aviation in the State of the applicant or operator.  Anti-icing  A procedure that provides protection against the formation of frost or ice and accumulation of snow on treated surfaces of the aeroplane for a limited period of time (hold-over time).  Approach Procedure with Vertical Guidance (APV) Operation  An instrument approach which utilizes lateral and vertical guidance, but does not meet the requirements established for precision approach and landing operations, with a decision height (DH) not lower than 250 feet and a runway visual range (RVR) of not less than 600 meters.  Cabin Crew Member  A crew member, other than a Flight Crew Member, who performs in the interests of safety of passengers, duties assigned by the operator or the Commander of the aeroplane.  Ceiling  The height above the ground or water of the base of the lowest layer of cloud below 6,000 meters (20,000 feet) covering more than half of the sky.  Circling  The visual phase of an instrument approach to bring an aeroplane into position for landing on a runway that is not suitably located for a straight-in approach.  Cloud Base  The height of the base of the lowest observed or forecast cloud element in the vicinity of an aerodrome or operating site or within a specified area of operations, normally measured above aerodrome elevation or, in the case of offshore operations, above mean sea level.  CM1  Flight crew member occupying left hand seat.  CM2  Flight crew member occupying right hand seat.  Commander  Pilot designated by Turkish Airlines or by the designated commander as being in command and charged with the safe conduct of the flight for the purpose of commercial air transport operations.  Co-pilot  A pilot serving in any piloting capacity other than as pilot-in-command or commander, but excluding a pilot who is on board the aircraft for the sole purpose of receiving flight instruction for a licence or rating.  Crew Member  A person assigned by an operator to perform duty on an aircraft during flight time.  Critical Phase of Flight  Critical phases of flight’ in the case of aeroplanes means the take-off run, the take-off flight path, the final approach, the missed approach, the landing, including the landing roll, and any other phases of flight as determined by the Commander. A list established for a particular aircraft type by the manufacturer with the approval of the State of Manufacture containing items, one or more of which is permitted to be unserviceable at the commencement of a flight. The MMEL may be associated with special operating conditions, limitations or procedures.  Minimum Descent Altitude (MDA) or Minimum Descent Height (MDH)  MDA or MDH means a specified altitude or height in a 2D instrument approach operation or circling approach operation below which descent must not be made without the required visual reference.  Minimum Equipment List (MEL)  A list, which provides for the operation of aeroplane, subject to specified conditions, with particular equipment inoperative, prepared by the operator in conformity with, or more restrictive than, the MMEL established for the aeroplane type.  Night  The hours between the end of evening civil twilight and the beginning of morning civil twilight or such other period between sunset and sunrise as may be prescribed by the appropriate authority.  Operational Control  The exercise of authority over the initiation, continuation, diversion or termination of a flight in the interest of the safety of the flight.  Performance Class A Aeroplanes  Multi-engine aeroplanes powered by turbo-propeller engines with a maximum operational passenger seating configuration of more than nine or maximum takeoff mass exceeding 5,700 kg, and all multi-engine turbo-jet powered aeroplanes.  Pilot Flying (PF)  A pilot who, for the time being, is in charge of the controls of an aircraft.  Pilot Monitoring (PM)  A pilot who is assisting the Pilot Flying in accordance with the multi-crew cooperation concept, when the required flight crew is more than one. Precision Approach  An instrument approach using Instrument Landing System, Microwave Landing System or Precision Approach Radar for guidance in both azimuth and elevation.  Runway Excursion  A runway excursion event occurs when an aircraft on the runway surface departs the end or side of the runway surface during takeoff or landing. They consist of two types of events: Veer Off: A runway excursion in which an aircraft departs the side of a runway. Overrun: A runway excursion in which an aircraft departs. It excludes both accidents where the aircraft did not initially land on a runway surface, and takeoff excursions that did not start on a runway (e.g., inadvertent take-offs from taxiways).  Runway Incursion  Any occurrence at an aerodrome involving the incorrect presence of an aircraft, vehicle or person on the protected area of a surface designated for the landing and take-off of aircraft. Objectives of Briefing  When conducting any briefing, the following objectives should be met: Define and communicate action plans and expectations under normal and non-normal conditions. Confirm applicable task sharing (i.e., crew members’ roles and responsibilities). Brief each subject area to its appropriate level of detail. Promote questioning and feedback. Ensure full understanding and agreement on the correct sequence of actions. Communicate objectives to cabin crew members and develop synergy. Enhance the preparedness of flight crew and cabin crew members for facing unusual requirements or responding to unexpected conditions.  3.1.2.2. Techniques for Conducting Effective Briefing  The style and tone of the briefing play an important role; interactive briefings (i.e., confirming the agreement and understanding of the PM after each phase of the briefing) provides more effective and productive briefings than an uninterrupted recitation terminated by the final query; “Any question?”. Interactive briefings provide the PF and PM with an opportunity to correct each other (e.g., confirming the use of the correct and effective departure/approach chart, confirming the correct setup of navaids for the assigned take-off/landing runway, etc.). This opportunity to correct each other is an important purpose of briefings. The briefing should encourage effective listening to attract the each other’s attention. The briefing should therefore be conducted when the workload and availability of the PM permit an effective communication. Any aspect that may affect normal operation (e.g., system failures, weather conditions or other particular conditions) should be carefully evaluated and discussed. Taxiing should be treated as a safety-critical activity due to the risks related to the movement of the aeroplane and the potential for a catastrophic event on the ground. Taxiing is a high workload phase of flight that requires the full attention of the flight crew. The final authority and responsibility of safe movement of the aeroplane rests with the commander. Flight crew shall maintain the sterile flight crew compartment procedures at all times during taxi. PF and PM should maintainan external look-out at all times during taxi. Continuous Descent Final Approach (CDFA)  A specific technique for flying the final approach segment of a non-precision instrument approach procedure as a continuous descent, without level-off, from an altitude/height at or above the final approach fix altitude/height to a point approximately 15m (50 ft) above the landing runway threshold or the point where the flare maneuver should begin for the type of aeroplane flown. Parallel Runway Operation  Refer to FCOM A319/A320/A321 PRO-SPO General situational and positional awareness is essential for both departing and landing flight crew. Absence of awareness might lead to taking off from or landing on parallel runways or taxiways. Final step for preventing such events is often a positive final check by the flight crew of the airplane orientation to the intended runway. While on departure, taxiing down a parallel runway or taxiway for the intended departure runway might lead to take off from wrong runway/ taxiway or incursions. The risk factor increases if the take-off clearance includes a runway change or to expedite the departure. Also delayed flights and rushed operations are prone to errors. Flight crews must ensure “heads up” operation and proper briefing is done to avoid such incidents. Non-precision approach to a runway, parallel to an adjacent runway or taxiway, especially in night time condition includes a risk factor and could lead to incursions. Offset, letdown or conventional VOR approaches, NDB approaches, RNAV/RNP approaches, visual approaches and circle to land approaches should be strictly monitored and orientation of the aircraft must be ensured that the airplane is in correct path and bearing from the intended runway. Proper lighting in the cockpit is essential and crosscheck with the external references such as runway markings and approach lighting systems should be made by pilots. Consider following: During the arrival briefing, examine the charts thoroughly, At FAF, call ATC or tower to turn on the approach lights, Before reaching MAP, confirm and call the runway in sight and identified, Use Runway Awareness and Advisory System (RAAS) (if applicable) to identify the correct runway. Use the MCDU functions of FMS such as FIX INFO to increase awareness and to minimize the risk factors that could lead to landing on parallel taxiways or runways. If either pilot is not sure of the landing runway, execute GO AROUND. Driftdown  Refer to FCOM A319/A320/A321 EFB Refer to FCTM A319/A320/A321 PR-AEP-ENG (Engine Failure During Cruise) Refer to QRH A319/A320/A321 PER Enroute performance requirements address the aeroplane capabilities in case of an engine failure in the enroute phase of flight. If an engine fails during cruise flight, the required thrust to maintain the altitude becomes greater than the available MCT of the remaining engine. The aeroplane has to descend to a lower altitude where, due to the higher density, the remaining engine can produce more thrust and is able to equal the required thrust to compensate the drag. The optimum driftdown speed offers the best lift-to-drag ratio and should be used in cases where staying as high as possible for as long as possible is desirable due to terrain or weather concerns. Using a higher than optimum driftdown speed will result in a slightly steeper descent path and a lower level-off altitude. MASS and BALANCE  7.1. Definitions  Basic Empty Weight (BEW)  Basic Empty Weight in weighing stated in weighing report items in an actual weight of the aircraft at the time of weighing. The Basic Empty Weight serves as a basis for the calculation of BASIC WEIGHT and BASIC CG of the aircraft in service.  The Delivery Empty Weight   is the scale weight adjusted for all items installed or removed at the time of weighing. And it may be used for delivery flights if any items are not installed after weighing.  Basic Weight (BW)  The weight of the aircraft with all its basic equipment plus a declared quantity of unusable fuel/oil. Also, apply missing equipment which is non-installed equipment on the aircraft shown in the weighing checklist. Basic weight includes: Potable water tanks full (100%) and lavatory chemicals, Removable passenger service equipment; and Flight deck and aircraft documents, passenger emergency cards and Skylife magazines, etc. Tare empty weight of all trolleys and galley equipment (hot-cup, hot-jug, etc.) Note: Note  Note:  If some items or equipment are not carried make necessary adjustments.  Dry Operating Weight (DOW)  The total weight of the aeroplane ready for a specific type of operation excluding all usable fuel and traffic load.   The weight includes such items as (figure-16) : Flight crew and cabin crew weights Catering (Pantry) weight E K . 1 0 . 7 3 . 0 0 5 | R e v . 0 7 | V . 0 0 O p e r a t i o n s M a n u a l P a r t - B A i r b u s 3 2 0 QMS Document Template Number: FR.18.0001 | Revision 12 Document Template Reference Document Number: TL.18.006 Page: 96 | Total Page: 109

Potable water adjustment if potable water tanks are not full (100%). Dry Operating Weight does not include any equipment in compartment (EIC) so it should not be added to DOW. It should be added in and shown as “Load in Compartments” (distribution) on Load and Trim Sheet as “Equipment in Compartment (EIC)”.  Operating Weight (OW)  It is the dry operating weight plus fuel but without traffic load.  Traffic Load  The total weight of passengers, baggage and freight.  Zero Fuel Weight (ZFW)  The total weight of the dry operating weight plus the traffic load.  Maximum Zero Fuel Weight (MZFW)  The maximum permissible weight of an aeroplane with no usable fuel. The weight of the fuel contained in the zero fuel weight when it is explicitly mentioned in the Load and Trim Sheets.  Maximum Structural Landing Weight (MLW)  The maximum permissible total aeroplane weight upon landing under normal circumstances.  Maximum Structural Ramp/Taxi Weight  The maximum approved weight for commencement of ground maneuvers. A mass greater than the maximum takeoff weight, to allow for fuel used in start-up and taxi.  Takeoff Weight (TOW)  The sum of operating weight, traffic load and fuel.  Maximum Structural Takeoff Weight (MTOW)  The maximum permissible total aircraft weight at the start of the takeoff run.  Centre of Gravity (CG)  The point through which the force of gravity is said to act a weight (in C of G terms, the point on the aircraft through which the total weight is said to act in a vertically downward manner). The Centre of Gravity is also the point of balance and such it affects the stability of the aircraft both on the ground and in the air.  Centre of Gravity Limits  The most forward and most aft positions of the CG at which the aircraft is permitted to fly.  Centre of Gravity Datum  The point on the aircraft designated by the manufacturer from which all measurements are taken in the calculation of the CG. The CG limits are defined relative to the datum. Sometimes the CG is additionally specified as a percentage of the Mean Aerodynamic Chord (MAC %).  Basic Empty Index (BI)  The index for the position of the center of gravity at basic empty weight.  Dry Operating Index (DOI)  The index for the position of the center of gravity at dry operating weight. Dry Operating Weight does not include any equipment in compartment (EIC) so it should not be added to DOI. It should be added in and shown as “Load in Compartments” (distribution) on Load and Trim Sheet as “Equipment in Compartment (EIC)”. 12.2. Evacuation Procedures 12.2.1. Preparation  When an abnormal situation develops, after airplane is stopped on the ground, set the parking brake and advise the cabin. This is done with a pre-warning call: “ ATTENTION CABIN CREW AT STATIONS ”. CM1 performs this pre-warning call before any decision is taken. The cabin crew expects this call as they are not always “ AT STATION ” during all ground phases.  12.2.2. Chain of Command  Final decision for Evacuation rests with the Commander. CM1 is responsible to start the evacuation process. Should it be physically impossible for the CM1 to carry out his duties, First Officer/CM2 will substitute for him according to the chain of command. Should the CM1 or First Officer/CM2 fail to inform cabin crew at this stage, Cabin Chief should try to contact any flight crew to initiate the evacuation; if not any other available cabin crew member shall initiate the evacuation by opening the exit that is responsible for.  When to you evacuate?  It is the responsibility of the Captain/CM1 to assess the situation and execute sound judgment to determine the safest course of action: In some cases, the application of the appropriate abnormal or emergency checklist will solve the problem. The worst-case scenario (explosion, fire crash landing) may not permit delayed evacuation. In this case the pre-warning call may be omitted. It may be difficult to assess the gravity of a situation. In some cases (e.g. fire) a confirmation of the emergency situation could be performed through the ATC or cabin report. Note: Warning  Warning:  As soon as the evacuation order is triggered, do not forget that this decision is irreversible. Time spent assessing the situation should be kept to a minimum to improve chances of survival if an evacuation is needed.  Decision not to evacuate  When the situation is considered safe, Commander decides in agreement with other crew member(s) (Captain/First Officer) not to evacuate. CM1 will advise the cabin crew using the public address: “ CABIN CREW AND PASSENGERS REMAIN SEATED ”. Then Commander/CM1 can make an additional explanatory announcement if necessary.  12.2.3. ATC call when evacuating  Refer to FCTM A319/A320/A321 PR-AEP-MISC (Evacuation Procedure) Captain/CM1 will call ATC after evacuation decision is made. The following call is suggested: “ MAYDAY, MAYDAY, MAYDAY, Turkish____, evacuation on____ ” (give position). Stating the position improves communications at a crucial moment.  12.2.4. Commander Duties when Leaving the Flight Deck  After completion of the abnormal or emergency checklist the commander will: Wear a complete uniform (for easy recognition) Pick up AML if time allows, Assist passengers and cabin crew in the cabin to evacuate, using reasonable judgment and without undue danger: check every row till the end of the cabin and assist immobilized passengers (if not yet done by the cabin crew), then evacuate using an aft exit or any other available exit, Identify himself to the authorities and ask for assistance before making declarations.  12.2.6. Rapid Deplaning (RD)  Refer to   EK.10.73.001 Operations Manual Part-A Chapter 8.2.2.1.8 Rapid Deplaning There may be a situation when an evacuation is not required but when passengers and crew should be deplaned immediately and quickly. In this instance rapid deplaning would be appropriate. Success   of   RD   depends   on   good   briefings   and   clear   passenger   announcements.   Cockpit   and   cabin   communication   with   the passengers should be clear and concise to ensure control and order. If possible, prior to RD, Commander will brief the Cabin Chief/Purser on doors to be used, availability of steps or bridge. In such a case, the flight crew make the following announcement: “ATTENTION CABIN CREW AT STATIONS.” If the flight crew has decided on the rapid deplaning, the flight crew shall give the following command: “ATTENTION CABIN CREW DISEMBARK THE AIRCRAFT.” If the rapid deplaning is not necessary, the flight crew give the following command: “CABIN CREW AND PASSENGERS REMAIN SEATED.”  13.
